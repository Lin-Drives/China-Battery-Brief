On September 17, the European Union put a number on the table — not a tariff rate, but a volume. The bloc is asking China to voluntarily limit hybrid vehicle exports to Europe, and to expect higher tariffs if it does not, the Financial Times reported. One day later, China's commerce ministry gave its answer in writing: so-called voluntary export restrictions seriously violate WTO rules, run counter to market principles and fair competition, and China firmly opposes them.[^1][^2]

The number underneath the ask explains its urgency. EU imports of hybrid vehicles from China rose from 3,800 in October 2024 to 50,000 in July 2026 — a thirteen-fold increase in twenty-one months — while average prices fell. In Brussels, the political framing has already been set: European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen said last week that the EU's trade deficit with China, running at about €1 billion a day, had reached a tipping point.[^1]

This is the second time Brussels has moved against a category of Chinese cars. The first time, it taxed them. In October 2024 the EU imposed countervailing duties on China-made battery-electric vehicles, taking the total tariff on a BEV to as much as roughly 45%, depending on the maker. But the regulation covered only cars driven exclusively by an electric motor. Hybrids — plug-in and otherwise — continued to pay the standard 10% import duty. The wall went up with a lane left open, and China's car industry changed lanes.[^1][^4]

## The lane marked 10%

The traffic data reads like a routing problem solved in real time. In 2025, China's plug-in hybrid exports to the EU grew 155% year on year; its BEV exports grew 12%. In the first quarter of 2026, Chinese makers shipped 438,000 vehicles to Europe, up 84.7% — of which 198,000 were BEVs, up 94.6%, and 106,000 were plug-in hybrids, up 152.4%. By April, Chinese brands held close to 30% of Europe's plug-in hybrid market, against 15.2% in BEVs, according to registration data compiled by Schmidt Automotive Research, a Berlin-based auto market researcher, and cited by the Nikkei.[^4]

The leaderboard tells the same story at model level. The best-selling plug-in hybrid in Europe in 2025 was the BYD Seal U — the export version of BYD's Song Plus SUV — with 65,900 registrations, priced below the Kia Sportage it competes with. In the first half of 2026 the three best-selling PHEVs in Europe were all Chinese: the Seal U, followed by the smaller BYD Atto 2 and the Jaecoo 7, the export brand of Chinese carmaker Chery, which launched in Europe only last year. The Volkswagen Tiguan, once the category's default choice, was pushed off the podium; Volkswagen's leadership has since lobbied publicly for higher tariffs.[^4][^5]

Individual markets show the flip in miniature. From January to May, BYD's plug-in hybrid registrations in the EU rose 260%, against 78% for its BEVs; of the more than 6,000 cars BYD registered in Germany in May, 70% were plug-in hybrids. Chery's PHEV deliveries topped 28,000 in a single month, up 668% year on year. Ferdinand Dudenhöffer, director of CAR, a German automotive research institute, estimates Chinese brands will sell about 800,000 cars in the EU this year and pass one million in 2027 — with hybrids doing the opening.[^4]

## Voluntary, in the Japanese sense

The instrument Brussels is reaching for has a history, and the history is the point. A voluntary export restraint is a quota the exporting country administers against itself, under the threat of worse. The template is the 1981 arrangement that capped Japanese car exports to the United States — negotiated, technically voluntary, and remembered in Tokyo as neither. The Financial Times reports that the EU hopes a restraint on hybrids could push Chinese carmakers to invest in Europe or partner with local manufacturers, echoing what the Japanese eventually did.[^1]

The ask also has a timetable. EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič was scheduled to speak with Chinese commerce minister Wang Wentao on September 18 and is expected in Beijing in the second week of October; the bloc has been asking since June for "tangible results" in rebalancing the trade relationship by October. And there is a stick already built behind the ask: Germany's Handelsblatt reported in June that the European Commission had completed preparatory work on countervailing duties for China-made plug-in hybrids — at rates mirroring the BEV schedule — and that the measure awaited only member-state approval. The voluntary restraint is the off-ramp offered before that file is opened.[^1][^4]

Beijing's refusal, delivered on September 18, was framed in law rather than arithmetic. Any solution between China and the EU, the commerce ministry said, must balance both sides' interests, comply with WTO rules and their respective domestic laws, and accommodate both industries. Voluntary export restraints sit outside WTO legality precisely because they bypass its disciplines on quantitative restrictions — which is why Brussels wants the cap to arrive carrying China's own signature.[^2][^3]

## The cap is meant to become a factory

Read the ask against the investment map and its purpose clarifies. Chinese carmakers have already announced European production plans totalling more than two million vehicles a year: BYD's wholly owned plant in Szeged, Hungary, with capacity for 300,000 cars, is set to start assembly this quarter; SAIC, the Chinese state-owned carmaker, has picked Galicia in Spain for a 120,000-unit plant due in 2028; Chery is building with Spanish partner Ebro at the former Nissan plant in Barcelona while contract-building at Nissan's Sunderland site in Britain; Leapmotor, backed by Stellantis, begins production of its B10 at the group's Zaragoza plant in October. A restraint that bites on shipped volumes raises the relative return on every one of these projects.[^4]

Dudenhöffer's verdict on the last round of tariffs is the argument Brussels is now testing: the duties did not protect Europe's industry, they accelerated the arrival of Chinese factories on European soil, faster than the Japanese or Koreans ever moved. A volume cap is designed to force the same migration on a deadline — and, unlike a tariff, it cannot be diluted by a cheaper car.[^4]

For the battery industry, the lane question matters beyond cars. Every hybrid that clears a European port carries a small Chinese-made battery; every hybrid built in Szeged or Zaragoza pulls demand toward the cell plants Chinese capital has already built in Europe — the capacity that sold out in Debrecen (No. 047). Closing the shipping lane does not close the battery flow. It reroutes it onto European soil, under European rules.[^5]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: quantity is the new price

Two years of this contest have produced a clean pattern. Round one was price: the EU taxed BEVs up to 45%, and Chinese exports of them kept growing, modestly. Round two was the loophole: the industry redirected into the untaxed category and grew it thirteen-fold. Round three opens with the EU abandoning the price instrument for the quantity instrument — a cap that no discount can undercut, because it counts cars rather than taxing them. Beijing's WTO objection is legally sound and, historically, beside the point: the 1981 restraint on Japan was just as contested, and it held for over a decade.[^1][^2]

The more durable lesson sits in what the cap is for. Brussels does not ultimately want fewer Chinese hybrids; it wants them built in Europe, with European labor and European supplier content — and it is now willing to ration market access to get them. That logic is familiar to readers of this newsletter from the other direction: Japan's capacity market rations foreign battery cells at 30% (No. 051), and Washington rations subsidies by content (No. 045). Europe is rationing the cars themselves. The ten-percent lane is closing. What replaces it is not a wall but a toll booth with a single accepted currency: local factories. Chinese carmakers, having announced two million units of European capacity, arrived at that conclusion before the EU did.[^4]
