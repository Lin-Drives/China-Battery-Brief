From September 23 to 25, Xi Jinping made his first state visit to the United States in eleven years — a tarmac greeting at Joint Base Andrews, a state dinner at the White House, and, on the final morning, a tea at which the two presidents agreed to build a "constructive relationship of strategic stability based on respect, fairness and reciprocity."[^1][^13] The deliverables arrived as an eight-point consensus: a Board of Trade, an Investment Council, an AI dialogue, fentanyl cooperation, and a pair of pandas for the Atlanta zoo.[^1] For the battery industry, the most telling feature of that list is, once again, an absence. Last issue it was a five-year plan with no output target (No. 056); this time it is a summit with no batteries. Across eight points and two days of choreography, batteries, electric vehicles and energy storage appear zero times.[^1][^2]

## The goods that made the list

What did get traded is precise. Under a "$30 billion for $30 billion" framework, the US will lower tariffs on roughly $30 billion of Chinese imports — toys, home appliances, baby products, kitchen and bath goods, holiday gifts — while China lowers tariffs on roughly $30 billion of American farm products, personal-care items, medical devices and coal. More than 90% of each list drops all of the mutually imposed additional duties and returns to most-favoured-nation rates, once both sides complete their domestic procedures.[^2] The lists are the message: relief is reserved for goods that neither side considers sensitive.

The one energy item in the package points the same direction. China agreed to buy 10 million tonnes of US coal a year in 2027 and 2028 — the pledge Bloomberg called the summit's "most specific," and an easy one to keep, at about 2% of China's annual coal imports.[^5] Coal made the list; the technologies of the energy transition did not. Climate was not mentioned at all: Belinda Schaepe, China lead at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA), a Helsinki-based think tank, read the silence as a signal that China is "looking for partners on this elsewhere."[^5]

## What stayed frozen

Everything that actually governs the battery trade was left untouched. On the US side, the tariff stack stands exactly where the 2024 Section 301 review put it: 100% on Chinese electric vehicles, 25% on lithium-ion EV batteries, and — since January 1 this year — 25% on non-automotive lithium batteries, which is to say storage cells, plus 25% on natural graphite and permanent magnets.[^7] None of it was on the table; the tariff-cut lists were built from goods that had never carried those duties.

On the Chinese side, the leverage is equally intact. On October 9, 2025, China's commerce ministry and customs administration issued Announcement No. 58, putting rechargeable lithium cells and packs above 300 Wh/kg, key cathode materials, artificial-graphite anode materials and the machines that make them — winding, stacking and formation equipment — under export licensing. Companion announcements the same day covered medium and heavy rare earths (No. 57) and created an extraterritorial rule reaching foreign-made products with as little as 0.1% Chinese rare-earth content (No. 61).[^9] A month later, Announcement No. 70 suspended all of it until November 10, 2026, as China's part of the Kuala Lumpur trade arrangement.[^10] Suspended — not withdrawn. The machinery of control is loaded and licensed, merely switched off.

This is what the US side failed to move. Restoring a regularised flow of critical minerals was, by Brookings' account, a principal American objective; the summit readout offers "no details or sense of movement" on it.[^4] Rare-earth leverage had already shaped the run-up: Reuters reported before the visit that China's near-total control of the minerals forced the Trump administration into a less hostile posture.[^6] The lever worked to calm the summit. It was not surrendered at it.

<!-- k:#5A6B9E|PART 03 · THE DOOR -->
## The door left open

One clause in the fine print does move the battery file. The two sides extended the Kuala Lumpur arrangement — the mutual pause on selected tariffs and non-tariff measures — from November 10, 2026 to January 10, 2027.[^3] Because Announcement No. 70's suspension of the battery, graphite and rare-earth controls was issued to implement that arrangement, extending the arrangement extends the pause: the controls stay off until January 10.[^10] That is now the single most important date on the industry's calendar.

The second opening is institutional. The new Investment Council gives the two governments a standing channel to discuss "potential investment opportunities and obstacles" — the first such mechanism for Chinese investment in the US in years.[^3] Going into the summit, the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), a Washington-based think tank, had floated the possibility that the White House would approve Chinese investments in electric vehicles, batteries and renewable energy.[^8] No approval came. But the template for one already exists, and it is running: Ford's wholly-owned plant in Marshall, Michigan, licensed to use CATL's LFP technology — the license-royalty-service (LRS) structure that keeps Chinese ownership at zero (No. 045) — was scaled from a $3.5 billion, 35 GWh plan to 20 GWh, and starts production this year, with first cells destined for a midsize electric pickup in 2027.[^11] Ford has since extended the licensed chemistry to stationary storage, and its new storage unit, Ford Energy, has signed a five-year framework with EDF power solutions for up to 20 GWh.[^11][^12] Whether such structures can keep their US tax credits under the prohibited-foreign-entity rules (No. 045) is precisely the kind of question a council exists to carry — even if this one has not carried it yet.

The calendar now does the negotiating. The AI dialogue holds its next round by the end of November; China hosts APEC in Shenzhen in November, with both leaders intending to attend each other's summits; the G20 meets in Miami in December; and on January 10 the extended truce expires.[^1][^3][^4] Two more leader-level meetings sit between today and the deadline.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: not a chip, a pause

Read the eight points as a map of what the two governments consider tradable, and batteries sit outside it in both directions. For the US, they are sensitive goods — the tariff relief was engineered to exclude them. For China, they are leverage — the export controls were suspended, never repealed, ready to be switched back on. Neither side tried to trade what it might still need to withhold.

What the industry actually received is a season of predictability, not relief. Every rule that matters — the 100% EV tariff, the 25% battery rates, the suspended controls, the contested tax credits — holds still until at least January 10, with APEC in November and the G20 in December as the two occasions where the pause gets rolled over or doesn't. For a company with US exposure, that is a planning window measured in weeks and meetings, not years. And it is only half of the season: the EU's trade commissioner, Maroš Šefčovič, is due in China with a hybrid-quota file in hand (No. 055). The battery industry spent the summer being regulated. This autumn, it is being negotiated about — on two fronts, in rooms it does not sit in.
