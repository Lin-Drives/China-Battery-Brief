On September 28, China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) and six other government agencies released the battery industry's first national special plan — a document dated September 14 that sets the course for 2026 through 2030. Its most telling feature is an absence. The plan sets no production target: not a gigawatt-hour of capacity, not a yuan of output value. It offers exactly three hard numbers, and all three are technical — all-solid-state batteries in initial large-scale application by 2030, a 15,000-cycle lifespan for long-life lithium cells, and parts-per-billion (PPB) defect rates for leading manufacturers.[^1][^2]

The framing is as deliberate as the metrics. Nan Cewen, an academician of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and a professor at Tsinghua University, called the document the country's first national-level special plan for the battery sector, and described the upgrade in status it implies: batteries are no longer a component but the base layer of electrification across industries — energy storage, low-altitude aircraft, robots. MIIT's own interpretation of the plan supplies both the scoreboard and the worry. Over the 14th Five-Year Plan period, it says, the industry's total output value topped 1 trillion yuan and took a major share of the global market; mass-produced cells passed 300 Wh/kg, and the longest-lived cells exceeded 12,000 cycles. The problems it names are not ones of scale, but "weak original innovation" and a supply-demand imbalance in lithium batteries.[^2][^3]

## Three metrics, no tonnage

The plan is built as five domains, 19 tasks and five annex-like "columns." For all that architecture, the 2030 targets that can be counted are just the three above. Read them against the baseline MIIT itself cites, and the shape of the ambition comes into focus. The cycle-life goal — 15,000 — is a roughly 25% extension of a record the industry has already reached; it is a reliability target, not a leap. The PPB defect rate is the sharpest of the three. One part per billion is 1,000 times stricter than the parts-per-million (PPM) ceiling that has governed mass manufacturing for decades: on a 150-cell pack, moving from PPM to PPB lifts pack-level yield from 99.985% — roughly one potentially defective car in 7,000 — to 99.999985%, or about one in seven million. Today only a few leading makers reach it, on selected lines, which is why the plan sets the 2030 target on "leading firms" rather than the industry as a whole.[^5] And the headline goal — all-solid-state batteries in "initial large-scale application" by 2030 — is phrased with a hedge built in. Initial. Large-scale application. Not mass production, not "on the road."[^2]

![A chart comparing PPM (1 in 1,000,000) and PPB (1 in 1,000,000,000) defect rates — a 1,000× gap — with a 150-cell pack's yield rising from 99.985% to 99.999985%](/figures/056-ppb-vs-ppm.svg "Figure 01 · Defect rate from PPM to PPB: a 1,000× tighter ceiling")

That wording is the plan's sharpest signal. For two years the battery industry's loudest timeline has been corporate: solid-state in 2027, in small volumes, in premium cars (No. 046). The state's own roadmap now places "initial scale application" three years later. The plan does not dismiss the 2027 talk; it declines to ratify it, and sets the bar at a phrase that can be met by pilot lines and first commercial batches rather than by a consumer product cycle. It also names the hard physics in between — interfacial contact, ionic conductivity, cycle stability, engineered pressure systems — and the materials that must arrive with them: high-voltage, high-capacity cathodes paired with lithium-metal or anode-free designs.[^1]

## A plan to slow an industry down

The most consequential task is not about the future at all. It is about the present. MIIT's interpretation says the plan is meant to address "supply-demand structural contradictions in lithium batteries," and lists the instruments: capacity early-warning and adjustment, product-quality supervision, and the disciplining of price competition — the anti-involution campaign that has run through the industry since 2024 (No. 053). The plan also backs corporate mergers and restructuring, calls for a "differentiated and specialised" regional layout, and orders faster domestic exploration of lithium and cobalt, including salt-lake and lepidolite resources.[^2][^3]

For a sector that already holds the global majority, that combination is coherent: the danger is no longer being too small, but being too crowded, too cheap and too dependent on imported raw materials. The state's role shifts from midwife to referee. At the same time the plan keeps the pipeline of next-generation technologies open — sodium and flow batteries as complements to a lithium core, and a long list of speculative chemistries (multivalent-ion, metal-air and metal-sulfur, even nuclear batteries) held as options rather than bets.[^1]

## Going abroad, with a passport

Batteries have been China's most exported industrial story of the decade. The plan treats that as a task to be managed, not a trend to be cheered. Its fifth domain, "improving the quality and level of trade and investment cooperation," instructs the government to guide firms to invest abroad "in a diversified and orderly way," to strengthen their overseas safety and security, to build an "overseas comprehensive service system," and to pursue mutual-recognition agreements that lower technical barriers to trade.[^1]

Two clauses in that section deserve more attention than they will get. The first is on recycling: the plan tells firms to build used-battery "comprehensive utilisation" capacity in qualified main export markets — that is, to move part of the circular economy to the countries whose rules now demand it. The second is traceability: it calls for a digital identity system for electric-vehicle power batteries, for China to "explore an internationally accepted digital passport scheme," and for a carbon-footprint accounting system with a factor database whose data can be mutually recognised across borders.[^1][^2]

Both are direct answers to Europe. The EU's battery passport becomes mandatory in February 2027 (No. 045), and its carbon-footprint rules decide market access. Rather than wait to be measured by someone else's method, China is building its own passport, its own factor database and its own mutual-recognition track — while pushing its recyclers to be physically present where the rules bite. Standards, in this plan, are not soft power. They are the terms of trade.[^1]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: China's answer is a specification, not a target

Industrial plans are not obliged to set output targets. What sets this one apart is what it puts in their place: not a tonnage figure but three things a supplier must prove — all-solid-state batteries in initial scale application by 2030, 15,000-cycle cells, and PPB-level defect rates. An industry that already supplies the world's majority does not need permission to make more; it needs a direction, discipline for a two-year price war (No. 053), secure raw materials, and standards its customers must accept. That makes the plan less a growth target than China's answer to the rulebook being written around it: where the EU rations Chinese cars by volume (No. 055) and will soon demand a battery passport with European carbon data (No. 045), China responds by standardising the cell, the carbon accounting and the passport on its own terms. What the industry gets is rare for a five-year plan — a clear picture of the next five years: not how much it will make, but what it must prove.
