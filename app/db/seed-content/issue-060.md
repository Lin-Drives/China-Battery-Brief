The COINS Act — the Comprehensive Outbound Investment Security Act — is the law that turned America's outbound-investment screening from an executive-order programme into a statute. Congress passed it on December 18, 2025, as Subtitle C of the fiscal 2026 National Defense Authorization Act, and codified it at Title VIII of the Defense Production Act.[^2] On October 22 the Treasury published the proposed rules that would implement it, and used the occasion to add biotechnology to the list of covered technologies.[^1]

Batteries were not added. Neither was energy storage, nor any part of the supply chain that makes them.

Two days later, China's Commerce Ministry called the proposal a "discriminatory expansion" of investment restrictions against China, a "typical abuse of the national-security concept" that "violates the important consensus reached by the two heads of state," and said China reserves the right to respond.[^3]

The gap is easier to read once the tools are separated. America does not regulate Chinese batteries with one instrument. It uses five, each with its own statute, agency and direction: capital leaving the US, capital entering the US, goods leaving the US, goods entering the US, and eligibility for a US subsidy. Four of them reach a Chinese battery. The one that does not is the one this week's rule extends.

## Five tools, five directions

| Tool | Legal basis | What it governs | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outbound investment (COINS/OISP) | Defense Production Act Title VIII; COINS Act 2025 | US capital into Chinese technology | Capital out |
| CFIUS | FIRRMA 2018 | Foreign capital into US assets | Capital in |
| Export controls | Export Control Reform Act 2018 | Goods and technology leaving the US | Goods and technology out |
| Section 301 tariffs | Trade Act of 1974, Section 301 | Chinese goods entering the US | Goods in |
| FEOC / 45X | IRA 2022, OBBBA 2025 | Eligibility for US clean-energy credits | Subsidy |

The table is the argument. Five tools, five legal foundations, five agencies, five directions. Reading them as one wall is the most common mistake in coverage of US China policy, and it produces bad business plans: a company that prepares for the tariff discovers later that its inverter cannot be certified or its cells cannot claim the credit.

COINS sits in the top row, and it is the narrowest of the five. It began as an executive order. On August 9, 2023, the Outbound Order declared a national emergency over US investment in three categories of technology — semiconductors and microelectronics, quantum information, and artificial intelligence — and named China, Hong Kong and Macau as countries of concern.[^2] The Treasury issued the implementing final rule on October 28, 2024, effective January 2, 2025.[^2] Congress then wrote the programme into law at the end of 2025, and the October 22 proposal is the first rulemaking under the statute.

The mechanism is a technology list plus two transaction buckets. A deal is covered only if a US person invests in a covered foreign person engaged in a listed technology. Notifiable transactions must be reported to the Treasury; prohibited transactions are barred.[^1] The statute names five sectors — semiconductors and microelectronics, artificial intelligence, quantum information, high-performance computing and supercomputing, and hypersonics — and lets the Treasury add more, subject to congressional consultation and notice-and-comment rulemaking.[^1] The Treasury used that authority to add biotechnology. It did not use it to add batteries.

Two distinctions matter more than the details. First, COINS is not CFIUS. The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, created in its modern form by the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act of 2018, reviews foreign money coming into American companies and assets; COINS reviews American money going out.[^10] Same family of concern, opposite direction. Second, COINS is not export control. The Export Control Reform Act of 2018 governs the shipment of goods and the transfer of technology out of the United States, enforced by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security.[^11] COINS governs the movement of capital. A chip-design licence and an equity cheque are different objects, regulated by different statutes.

<!-- k:#5A6B9E|PART 02 · WHERE THE BATTERY SITS -->
## Outside the definition

Why is a battery not on the COINS list? Because the statute is aimed at technology that can be used for military modernisation, intelligence, surveillance or cyber-enabled capabilities.[^1] A lithium-iron-phosphate cell plant is a manufacturing asset. It matters enormously to the energy transition, and it is substitutable in a way that a semiconductor fab is not. It is not a technology the US fears will be transferred through a cap table.

That classification does not leave batteries unregulated. It routes them to the other four tools.

**Goods in.** The Section 301 review finalised in September 2024 raised tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles to 100% and on lithium-ion batteries to 25%, with the storage-cell rate taking effect in January 2026.[^6] China answered at the source: in October 2025 it put export controls on cells at or above 300 Wh/kg, on cathode materials, on artificial graphite anodes and on the equipment to make them, then suspended those controls until November 10, 2026.[^7][^8]

**Subsidy.** The foreign entity of concern, or FEOC, rules do not ban Chinese batteries. They decide whether a project using them can claim the credits that make it financeable, principally the investment tax credit under Section 48 and the advanced-manufacturing credit under Section 45X.[^9] Non-compliance eliminates the entire 30% investment tax credit base rate, not just the domestic-content adder. Developers at the 2026 US Battery Asset Management Summit put compliant battery cost at US$180–200 per kWh, against US$100–120 for the Chinese product they had been buying; some cut project duration from five hours to three to keep the numbers above their return threshold.[^5]

**Equipment.** On July 28 the Federal Communications Commission announced a ban on foreign-produced inverters from US power plants on national-security grounds. In late August the White House signed an executive order declaring a national emergency over foreign-made power equipment — grid-connected inverters, transformers and battery storage systems interacting with transmission lines of 69 kV or above, sourced from 24 countries on US arms-embargo and sanctions lists, China most pertinently.[^4] The order applies to deals initiated after August 26 and gives the Department of Energy 120 days to publish implementing rules, putting the deadline in late December.[^4]

![Diagram of four US regulatory layers applied to Chinese batteries — tariffs, subsidy eligibility, grid equipment and capital screening — with the capital layer marked as not covering batteries](/figures/060-control-stack.svg "Figure 01 · Three layers reach the battery; the fourth does not name it")

The equipment layer is the one that can stop a project rather than reprice it. Developers describe the inverter measure as more dangerous than FEOC compliance, because almost every modern inverter has an internet port, and a directive from the Department of War — the defence department's current name — has told the FCC that the ban covers wired inverters connecting via Ethernet.[^5] The FCC stopped answering industry questions after August 20.[^5] The US is also building substitutes at this layer: Spain's Power Electronics opened a Houston factory in September, and Flex, the contract electronics manufacturer, agreed to buy inverter maker EPC Power for US$4.4 billion the same month.[^12][^13]

Capital is the layer that stays open, and it matters less than it looks. A joint venture with a Chinese battery firm is not prohibited by COINS; it still faces case-by-case review where CFIUS applies, state-level legislation, political scrutiny of named companies, and the ownership-penetration test inside FEOC. The Ford–CATL arrangement — Ford, one of Detroit's big three carmakers, builds and owns the plant; CATL, China's largest battery maker, licenses the technology without an equity stake — was designed for FEOC, not for COINS, but it sits outside both (No. 048, No. 057). The missing layer removes one obstacle. It does not remove the other three.

Nor is the gap permanent. COINS lets the Treasury add technologies through rulemaking, and the comment period on this proposal closes on December 7.[^2] The question is not whether batteries will be added this year. It is whether the line between "strategic technology" and "strategic supply chain" moves again, as it has for three years.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: design for eligibility, not for tariffs

The US has not decided that Chinese ownership of a battery plant is a national-security threat. It has decided that Chinese batteries, Chinese inverters and Chinese cells inside a subsidised project are a supply-chain and cybersecurity problem. Those are different judgments, made by different agencies under different statutes, and they move at different speeds.

For a Chinese battery company, the practical consequence is that the binding constraints are eligibility and grid connection, not ownership and not the tariff line. The companies that have adapted fastest started from those two tests — which equipment can be certified, which ownership structure clears FEOC — and worked backwards to the factory. That is slower and more expensive than building in a third country to dodge a tariff, and it is why the US market is consolidating around a small number of bankable suppliers rather than opening to the lowest-cost one.

The capital carve-out is real. It is also the least useful fact in the file.

## Three dates

On November 10 China's suspension of its battery and graphite export controls expires. On December 7 the comment period on the COINS proposal closes. By late December the Department of Energy must publish rules implementing the late-August equipment order. Each date changes a different test: source, ownership, and connection.
