On October 16, the first European-built Leapmotor B10 rolled off the line at the Zaragoza plant of Stellantis, the carmaker that owns Peugeot, Citroën and Opel. The car belongs to Leapmotor, the Chinese EV maker in which Stellantis holds about 20%. A banner behind the stage read "Two forces. A bright electric future. Built in Europe." A day earlier, 30 km away in the town of Mallén, Leapmotor had marked the start of production at a battery workshop that supplies the packs for the same car.[^1][^2][^3]

Together the two sites make Leapmotor, by its own account, the first Chinese carmaker to assemble both vehicles and battery packs in Europe. They also make it the cleanest test yet of what "localisation" means under the EU's tariff-and-content regime. The car is built in Spain. The pack is built in Spain. The cells inside it still arrive from China.

That last fact is not in the press release. It comes from a Mallén engineer quoted by Economic Observer, a Chinese business newspaper: cells are shipped from China, and the Spanish plant assembles them into modules and packs.[^3] Leapmotor has not publicly named its European cell supplier. Zhu Jiangming, Leapmotor's founder, says first-phase localisation at Zaragoza will exceed 40%.[^3] The next threshold under discussion in Europe is 70%.

The gap between those two numbers is the story.

## Two plants, one knocked-down kit

The arrangement is a joint venture, not a wholly owned plant. Stellantis owns 51% of Leapmotor International, the entity with the exclusive rights to build and sell Leapmotor cars outside Greater China; Leapmotor holds the rest. Zaragoza supplies the factory, the supply chain and the dealers. Leapmotor supplies the platform and the process. The B10 is assembled from completely knocked-down (CKD) kits shipped out of China, on a line shared with the Opel Corsa, Peugeot 208 and Lancia Ypsilon.[^3]

The plant is not a greenfield bet. It has built cars for 43 years and produced more than 10 million of them, and it sits in Aragon, at the centre of a quadrilateral formed by Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and the Basque Country. Spain is the EU's second-largest car producer after Germany, with 2.27 million vehicles in 2025.[^3]

Volumes are modest by Chinese standards. Leapmotor plans 9,000 B10s at Zaragoza in 2026, with the B05 following in the first quarter of 2027 and an Opel C-SUV on Leapmotor's platform in 2028.[^3] European orders for the B10 reached more than 8,000 in September alone.[^3] The car starts at €29,900 in Europe, with a 56.2 kWh pack and 361 km of WLTP range, or a 67.1 kWh pack and 434 km.[^5]

Mallén is the more interesting bet. The workshop opened on June 23 and began production on October 15, seven months from setup to output, according to Economic Observer. The site covers 34,000 square metres, with 19,000 square metres of buildings; Reuters puts the line at about 65,000 packs a year with headroom to 100,000, while Chinese reports describe a first phase of 60,000 ramping to 100,000 and a second phase that could reach 200,000.[^2][^4][^3] It employs about 400 people, rising to 500 by year-end.[^3]

Set the pack line against the car line and the mismatch is deliberate. At 65,000 packs, Mallén can supply more than seven times the 9,000 cars Zaragoza plans to build in 2026. The line is sized for the B05, the Opel C-SUV and possibly outside customers, not for this year's B10.

Why build packs at all when CATL is building a 50 GWh cell plant next door? Economic Observer gives the answer: CATL's Spanish plant, a joint venture with Stellantis, does not yet match Leapmotor's timeline or product requirements.[^3] The cell plant broke ground in November 2025 with a target of starting production by the end of 2026 and a reported completion date of March 2028; its first phase is planned at up to 50 GWh for up to €4.1bn.[^10] Leapmotor needed packs in 2026.

## What the forty percent measures

"More than 40%" is a company figure with no published denominator. It could mean share of parts by value, by cost, by count, or by supplier origin. Leapmotor has not said. But the battery is where the arithmetic becomes awkward.

![Diagram of three localisation numbers: 40% company first-phase figure, 70% proposed EU component threshold from 2028, and 20–30% local value inside a pack assembled from imported cells](/figures/059-forty-percent.svg "Figure 01 · Three numbers, one question: 40% now, 70% proposed, and the 20–30% that stays in the pack")

Cells are the most valuable part of a pack. Industry estimates put cell cost at 70–80% of pack cost; the module and pack assembly that Mallén performs accounts for the rest. If that ratio holds, the Spanish content of a pack built from imported cells is 20–30%, before counting locally sourced housings, wiring or thermal parts. The pack is built in Spain. Most of its value is not.

Scale makes the point. At 56.2–67.1 kWh per B10 pack, 65,000 packs a year implies roughly 3.7–4.4 GWh of cell demand. CATL's planned Spanish plant is 50 GWh. Mallén is not a cell plant by another name; it is a pack plant sized at 7.3–8.7% of the cell capacity being built 30 km away.

Then there is the tariff. A China-built Leapmotor B10 pays 10% at the border plus a 20.7% countervailing duty, 30.7% in total, inside the 7.8–35.3% range the EU imposed in October 2024 and below the 45.3% ceiling.[^6] At a €29,900 list price, the difference is about €9,180 a car. That is the prize for assembling in Spain, and it is large enough to explain the speed of the project without any appeal to industrial strategy.

## From made in Europe to made of Europe

The tariff arithmetic only works if the car counts as EU-origin. That is not automatic. The EU's non-preferential origin rules require the "last substantial transformation"; minimal operations never confer origin, and processing intended to avoid tariff measures is treated as not economically justified.[^7] Assembling a CKD kit is more than screwing on a wheel, but it is also the kind of operation that invites an anti-circumvention inquiry if the value added is thin. No such inquiry into Leapmotor has been publicly announced. The risk is structural, not imminent.

The rules are also moving from the car to its contents. In March 2026 the European Commission proposed the Industrial Accelerator Act, which would attach conditions to foreign investment above €100m in batteries and electric vehicles: ownership capped at 49%, at least 50% EU employment, and commitments on technology transfer, local content and R&D.[^8] The proposal is not law.

A separate Franco-German push, reported on October 16, would go further: from 2028, vehicles sold with subsidies, low-cost leases or public procurement would need at least 70% of component value, excluding the battery, from European suppliers.[^9] That text still needs member states and the European Parliament. The battery exclusion is telling. The rule-writers know cells are the hardest part to localise, so they left them outside the 70% and will regulate them through the battery file instead, starting with the carbon-footprint declaration due in February 2027.[^3]

Read together, the direction is clear. The test is shifting from "made in Europe" to "made of Europe". A CKD kit plus an imported-cell pack clears the first test. It does not clear the second.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: tariff localisation, not supply-chain localisation

Leapmotor's Spanish model is the fastest, lightest way into the EU: borrow a plant, borrow a dealer network, build the pack yourself, keep the cells on a ship. It is a template for tariff localisation, not for supply-chain localisation, and it is rational for as long as the rules price the car rather than its contents.

The contrast with BYD is instructive. BYD is building whole plants in Hungary and looking at France; CATL built Debrecen and is building Zaragoza. Those are heavy, slow, capital-intensive bets that put cells on European soil. Leapmotor's bet is lighter and faster, and it has already produced a car. But the missing piece is the same one that determines whether the model survives the next rulebook: the cell.

The CATL-Stellantis joint venture could close the gap. If its Zaragoza plant supplies cells to the adjacent Leapmotor line, localisation moves from the pack to the cell, and 40% becomes a waypoint rather than a ceiling. Neither company has confirmed that supply relationship, and the timing does not yet match. Until it does, Mallén buys time.

Forty percent is a start. Seventy is the gate. The cell is the answer.

<!-- k:#F0A832|ON THE HORIZON -->
## Three dates

In the first quarter of 2027 the B05 joins the Zaragoza line, doubling the models that need packs. In February 2027 the EU's battery carbon-footprint rules begin to bite. In April 2027 China cuts the export rebate on new-energy vehicles from 13% to 9%, making local assembly more attractive still.[^3] By 2028, if the 70% rule survives the legislative process, the arithmetic of a CKD kit and an imported-cell pack will no longer close.
