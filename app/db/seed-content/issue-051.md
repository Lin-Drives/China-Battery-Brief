In late August, Rept Battero 瑞浦兰钧 — the Chinese cell maker controlled by the nickel giant Tsingshan 青山控股 — switched on its first grid-connected battery-storage project in Japan: 1.99 MW / 8.35 MWh in Kanuma, Tochigi Prefecture. Against a 461 GWh half, the volume is trivial. But it lands at the exact moment Japan is rewriting who gets paid to store power, and read against that rewrite the project stops being a small asset and starts being a probe. China's storage ceiling in Japan is not a chemistry problem. It is a policy line — and, per industry reporting, this month that line is drawn at thirty percent.[^1][^3]

<!-- k:#147A67|PART 01 · THE PROJECT -->
## A small asset, a rule-proof revenue stack

Kanuma is deliberately unambitious. The site is a standalone, independent grid-side storage plant using Rept's 417 kWh liquid-cooled outdoor DC cabinets, supplied as full cabinets by the company. It does not chase a single anchor contract; it stacks three revenue streams — Japan's frequency-regulation ancillary-services market, the capacity market, and the spot market — and, on the side, delivers BCP backup capacity and lifts power quality for the grid. Delivery follows the ecosystem's now-standard split: 80% global fixed standard, 20% local adaptation, with certified field engineers and local partners running FAT, SAT and commissioning. Rept says a growing pipeline of Japanese projects will follow, with more scheduled in September.[^1][^2]

Read the revenue stack, not the megawatts. Kanuma makes money on the two streams Japan has not gated, plus one that it hands out by auction. That is the tell for the whole story.

<!-- k:#B43A2B|PART 02 · THE RULE -->
## The rule: one number, no more than thirty percent

In May, Japan's transmission operators (OCCTO) announced the third round of the Long-Term Decarbonisation Power Sources Auction — the capacity market that hands a winner a 20-year fixed-revenue contract starting in FY2027. This round selected 19 battery projects totalling 1,251 MW, and it came with an eligibility floor of six hours: only projects planned for six hours of duration or more could bid, alongside a new JC-STAR cybersecurity certification. And then the clause that matters: domestic-content rules that, per industry reporting, mean no more than 30% of the awarded battery capacity may use cells manufactured in any single foreign country. For the first time in the storage story, the ceiling is not a tariff and not a performance target — it is a number written inside the allocation itself.[^3]

Six hours is the quiet constraint. It pushes the auction toward a different kind of chemistry and a different kind of cell — long-duration, high-cycle — and it stacks a local-content rule on top. Two doors close at once: the size of the prize, and who is allowed to walk through it.

<!-- k:#8A6110|PART 03 · THE CONTRAST -->
## Capital crosses the line. Cells do not.

The sharpest detail is who won. One of the named winners this round was CHC — a Singapore-based BESS developer whose investors include a joint venture backed by CATL 宁德时代 — with contracts of 75.6 MW and 36.7 MW. CHC had also partnered with US investor Stonepeak to take 348 MW in the previous round. CHC was behind the Matsuyama 12 MW/35.8 MWh grid-scale project in Ehime, with Shikoku Electric — the island's first. Chinese capital is inside the capacity market, and it is there as a developer, winning multi-decade revenue.[^3][^4]

But the cell is capped. The same auctions that let Chinese-backed developers sign for gigawatt-scale capacity will not let their cells take more than 30% of the awarded stack. That is the split the rest of the market has already read. Gotion 国轩高科 (the Chinese battery maker based in Hefei, among the first listed Tier-1 LFP producers) has been in Japan since 2017 and sells through Edison Power and a Daiwa Securities-backed investor targeting 1 GWh; Sungrow 阳光电源 (the Chinese inverter and energy-storage systems leader) signed with Sun Village, the Japanese developer backed by Marubeni, for around 500 MWh across Japan. All of these are supply or system-integration routes into the open, un-gated demand — not the 20-year capacity-market prize.[^5]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: the cap is the story

Last week this page argued the storage clock runs on policy. Japan is the cleanest proof. Chinese cell makers can build and ship — nobody disputes the capacity or the cost curve. What Japan is testing is whether that capacity converts into the most stable, longest-duration revenue in the market, and — per industry reporting — the answer is a hard 30%.

So the trade is not "China wins storage" or "China loses Japan." The trade is that Chinese firms will stop trying to ship cells into the capacity market and start chasing the demand that has no local-content cap — frequency regulation, spot arbitrage, C&I, residential — while putting capital, not cells, into the developers who bid the giant projects. That is the exact licensing pattern Europe produced: monetise through equity and IP, not through the anchor, most durable revenue stream. The project that tells you this is eight MWh big. The rule that makes it true is thirty percent wide.
