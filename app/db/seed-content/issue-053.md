The document is dated August 24. It appeared on the Ministry of Commerce's website on September 1. In the week between, three Beijing ministries — commerce, industry and information technology, and market regulation — put their names to a document with an unglamorous title and an unusual addressee: the "Guidelines for Overseas Competitive Conduct and Compliance Building in the Automotive Industry," circular 商合函〔2026〕451号. Four chapters, twenty articles, and a first for Chinese industrial policy: a rulebook written not for foreigners doing business in China, but for Chinese companies doing business everywhere else.[^1]

The timing is not subtle. China exported 8.32 million vehicles in 2025, to more than 200 countries and regions, and Chinese companies have invested in auto manufacturing in over 80. The momentum is still building: passenger-vehicle exports reached about 5.18 million in the first seven months of 2026, and within that, new-energy vehicles hit 540,000 units in July alone — up 147.8% year on year, and 58.8% of all passenger-vehicle exports. When your exporters grow that fast, their conduct stops being their problem and becomes yours.[^2][^3]

<!-- k:#2F6E77|PART 01 · THE DOCUMENT -->
## Twenty articles, and most of them are about price

Strip the document to its chassis and two-thirds of it is about money changing hands. Chapter two, on overseas market competition, reads like a list of everything that went wrong in China's domestic car market, translated into export instructions. Set prices on a cost basis, oriented to international supply and demand — do not disrupt market order for improper advantage (Article 4). When you set a suggested retail price abroad, build clear price tiers by configuration, and avoid frequent, steep price swings that burn consumers and your own brand (Article 5). Cross-border price differences between national markets should have a visible logic — taxes, logistics, local conditions (Article 6). Respect your overseas dealers' pricing autonomy, and set out sales incentives in clear, reasonable terms (Article 7). No hidden markups or unlisted fees on top of the sticker (Article 8). Promotions — gifts, financing offers, discounts — follow local law and local custom (Article 9). Advertising must be true, complete and not misleading (Article 10).[^1]

Chapter three is the compliance spine, and it is where the document reaches past marketing into the factory: full-process compliance and product-market-fit assessment, plus production safety and risk plans (Articles 11–12), quality management and after-sales networks (13), local labor law, employee rights and vocational training (14), and — the one with the longest shadow — connected-vehicle and autonomous-driving data: collection, use, protection and cross-border transfer, all to be handled in compliance with applicable rules (15). Intellectual property and antitrust get an article each; so does climate and supply-chain decarbonisation (16–18).[^1]

Then chapter four tells you what kind of document this is. Article 19: the guidelines are general guidance, offered "for reference" — 供企业参考. Article 20: interpretation rests with the three ministries. There is no penalty clause, no enforcement calendar, no blacklist. The teeth, if any, live elsewhere.[^1]

<!-- k:#B0432F|PART 02 · WHY NOW -->
## Beijing fought the price war at home. Now it is banning the export version

To understand why twenty articles of manners needed three ministries, look at the domestic campaign they extend. In late May 2025, the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers, the auto industry's trade association, issued an initiative against disorderly price-cutting, and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) publicly backed it — a senior official's phrase, widely quoted: a price war has no winners, and no future. By mid-2025 seventeen automakers had pledged to pay suppliers within 60 days, a direct attack on the habit of financing price cuts by stretching the supply chain. Industry margins tell the story: aggressive price-cutting had pressed the sector's average profit margin to a historic low of around 4%.[^6]

The campaign did not stop at prices. In July 2026, MIIT sat down with the key automakers and told them to look hard at production consistency, reliability, durability and the validation of new technology — the quality side of the same disease. And this week, as the guidelines circulated, Caixin, the Beijing financial news outlet, named the anxiety precisely in an editorial: 内卷外溢 — involution, spilling outward. The domestic disease of racing to the bottom, exported. China Automotive Technology and Research Center, the state-affiliated industry research body, supplied the official reading: the guidelines exist to prevent irrational competition from extending overseas, and to retire the extensive-growth model abroad.[^5]

That is the honest way to read the document. It is not a trade measure aimed at anyone abroad. It is Beijing telling its own champions: the playbook that won you the home market — cut price, squeeze dealers and suppliers, flood the zone — is exactly the playbook that will get you regulated, sued and expelled abroad. So the ban comes from home first.[^1][^5]

<!-- k:#5A6B9E|PART 03 · THE EU PARALLEL -->
## Brussels built a price floor from the outside; Beijing is writing one from the inside

There is a mirror across the Eurasian landmass. In October 2024, after its anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese-made electric vehicles, the European Commission imposed definitive countervailing duties ranging from 7.8% to 35.3%, on top of the standard 10% car tariff. Then, in January 2026, the Commission published a guidance document on price undertakings: Chinese exporters can offer binding minimum import prices — with rules on sales channels, cross-compensation and future EU investment — in place of the duties. Brussels' answer to Chinese price aggression was, literally, a price floor, negotiated firm by firm.[^4]

Now set the two documents side by side. The EU's says: you will not sell below a minimum price, or you will pay the duty. Beijing's says: you will not slash prices, whipsaw them, or squeeze your own dealers — because it is bad for you, and embarrassing for us. One is law with a tariff behind it; the other is guidance with nothing behind it but the ministries that sign your export paperwork. But they rhyme. After years of being accused of exporting deflation, China has started writing the discipline its trading partners were going to impose anyway.[^1][^4]

Regular readers can place this in the series. Japan rations Chinese cells by quota — a written 30% ceiling in its capacity market (No. 051). Australia rations by market — no wall at all, and spreads down 85% in a year (No. 052). Brussels rations by price floor. And now Beijing rations by conduct. Every major market Chinese electrification touches has found its own allocation mechanism; this week China unveiled its own. And note the scope: Article 2 covers the automotive industry chain, not just carmakers. The single most valuable component in that chain is the battery. What Brussels did to cars with a price floor, and what Beijing now does to cars with a conduct code, the battery industry should read as a preview — the EU battery passport already does the data side (No. 045).[^1][^4]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: first know-how, now behavior

A guidance document without penalties is easy to dismiss, and the dismissal would be wrong. Beijing has spent two years rationing what leaves China — first the technology, through export controls on battery know-how and materials; now the behavior, through twenty articles on how to price, promote and employ abroad. The direction of travel is consistent: the state is treating the overseas expansion of its champion industries as something it manages, not something it merely cheers.

For automakers the message is blunt. Cheap won the first phase — 8.32 million exports and factories on four continents. The second phase is licensed by conduct, and the license is issued in Beijing before it is checked in Brussels, Brasília or Bangkok. The price war was a domestic embarrassment. Exported, it becomes a diplomatic one. That is what 451号 is for: making sure the world's next argument with Chinese cars is about how good they are, not how cheap.
