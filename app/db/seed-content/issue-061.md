CATL's third-quarter report reads like a misprint. Revenue rose 58.6% from a year earlier to Rmb165.2bn. Net profit rose 14.5% to Rmb21.3bn. The next morning its Hong Kong shares fell 10.6% to their lowest since July 2025, and JPMorgan cut the stock to neutral.[^1][^2]

The market was not rejecting growth. It was repricing its quality. CATL, the world's largest battery maker, sold more cells than ever and earned less on each one: gross margin fell to 20.7% in the quarter, the second consecutive decline, and to 22.7% for the first nine months from 25.3% a year earlier.[^1] Three things shaped the quarter. China began levying a 2% consumption tax on lithium batteries on September 1. The export rebate on battery products had already been cut from 9% to 6% in April and will disappear on January 1, 2027. And the sales mix tilted further toward grid storage, where cells are sold into a more competitive, lower-margin market.[^1][^2]

That is the uncomfortable core of this earnings season. Demand is accelerating: BloombergNEF, the research arm of Bloomberg, now expects 188GW/535GWh of global storage additions in 2026, up 45% from 2025 and above its own mid-year forecast of 158GW/459GWh.[^3] The pivot to storage is real. It is not repairing margins. In many cases it is compressing them.

## Everyone grew. Almost no one kept it

The quarter produced a clean natural experiment: the same demand shock, applied to companies with different positions in the chain and different exposure to policy.

Eight of the nine grew revenue by more than 50%. Profit went in three directions.

CATL's problem is arithmetic. Revenue grew four times faster than net profit, and the gap showed up in the balance sheet as well as the income statement. Operating cash flow fell 23.6% in the first nine months to Rmb61.6bn, receivables rose 47.8% to Rmb112.9bn and inventories rose 53.2% to Rmb144.8bn. The company attributes both to business expansion. That is true, and it is also the point: growth at this stage consumes working capital faster than it produces earnings.[^1]

The Korean trio tells a different story. LG Energy Solution, Korea's largest battery maker, lifted operating profit 25.7% from a year earlier and 567% from a weak second quarter, on revenue of KRW9.64tn. But KRW416.9bn of the KRW756bn operating profit came from the US advanced-manufacturing production credit, known as AMPC; strip it out and the margin is 3.7%. The company still posted a net loss of KRW296.9bn for the quarter and a cumulative net loss of KRW1.57tn for the first nine months, and said fourth-quarter profitability faces start-up costs at its Honda joint venture.[^4][^5][^6]

Samsung SDI, Korea's second battery maker, delivered the quarter the market had been waiting for: revenue up 52.6%, operating profit of KRW407.8bn, its best in 12 quarters, and a return to profit. The driver was not cars. It was energy storage, especially batteries for AI data centres, and the company says its US ESS orders already cover output at StarPlus, its US joint venture with Stellantis, and its Ulsan lines through 2028. It began mass-producing high-capacity LFP cells at StarPlus in October.[^7]

SK On, the battery unit of SK Innovation, is the laggard. Revenue fell 23% and it stayed in operating loss at KRW80.5bn, though that was better than the KRW124.8bn loss a year earlier. Its answer is the same as everyone else's: convert car lines to storage. It will convert 3GWh of the 7GWh at its Seosan plant in Korea to ESS cells, with trial production by the end of 2026, and has a 9GWh LFP supply deal with NeoVolta, a US energy-storage project developer, starting in 2027.[^8]

Panasonic Energy, the battery arm of Panasonic, shows what a stable customer base is worth. First-half revenue rose 46.9% and operating profit nearly tripled, helped by North American EV battery sales and, increasingly, storage systems for data centres. The company raised its full-year Energy operating-profit forecast by 16% to ¥198bn.[^9]

The Chinese second tier is where the divergence becomes violent. EVE Energy, a Chinese cellmaker that is now one of the largest storage-cell suppliers, and CALB, a state-linked Chinese cellmaker, both converted revenue growth of more than 50% into profit growth of 60% to 97%, with EVE shipping 23.1GWh of storage cells in the quarter alone. Gotion High-tech, the Volkswagen-backed cell maker, grew revenue 74.6% but reported a Rmb537m net loss; strip out fair-value and currency swings and its adjusted profit was Rmb145m. Sunwoda, a Chinese battery maker, grew revenue 52.8% and reported net profit of Rmb10.6m, down 98%, because it deconsolidated its power-battery subsidiary, a move expected to add Rmb3.2bn–4.5bn to profit later.[^10][^11][^12][^13]

The pattern is not "good companies and bad companies". It is three different profit models hitting the same demand curve at once: a scale leader absorbing policy costs, two Korean incumbents monetising US eligibility, and a group of Chinese challengers whose reported earnings depend as much on accounting events as on cell margins.

## The boom is real. The order book is softer than it looks

The demand side is not in doubt. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, a London-based battery supply-chain researcher, counted 177GWh of grid-scale storage commissioned globally in the first eight months of 2026, up 35% year on year, with China accounting for 61% of August's volume.[^14] Inside China, the China Automotive Battery Innovation Alliance (CABIA) recorded 73.5GWh of storage-battery sales in September, up 104.4% year on year and equal to 33% of combined power- and storage-battery sales.[^1]

The order statistics are where the story gets slippery. CESA, the Energy Storage Application Branch of the China Industrial Association of Power Sources, an industry group whose database tracks company-disclosed overseas orders, counted 584.6GWh of overseas orders disclosed by 145 Chinese companies in the first nine months of 2026. The quarterly path looks explosive: 112.7GWh in the first quarter, 176GWh in the second, 295.9GWh in the third.[^15]

One deal explains most of the acceleration. On September 17, EVE Energy signed a 206GWh multi-year cell-supply agreement with Fluence, the US storage integrator. That single contract accounts for 35.2% of all disclosed capacity in the nine months. Remove it and third-quarter orders were 89.9GWh, down about 49% from the second quarter.[^15]

CESA is careful to say that this does not mean overseas demand fell. Announcement timing, contract duration and disclosure practice all distort quarterly comparisons. The more useful conclusion is that a single framework agreement can move an industry statistic by more than 200GWh, and that signed capacity is not delivered capacity, revenue or cash. The database also shows where the orders sit: the Americas at 236GWh, Europe at 135GWh, Asia at 116GWh, and 94% of the total in cells and systems rather than engineering or development.[^15]

![Bar chart of Chinese overseas storage orders by quarter in 2026, showing Q3 inflated by a single 206GWh EVE–Fluence agreement](/figures/061-order-curve.svg "Figure 01 · One contract bends the curve")

The export data adds a second caution. China exported US$78.8bn of lithium-ion batteries in the first nine months, up 42.2% by value and 26.4% by volume. But exports to the United States fell 17.4% to US$7.36bn, while exports to the Netherlands rose 89.3% to US$5.71bn; the association attributes the Dutch jump to storage cells transiting Rotterdam.[^16] Value is growing faster than volume, which is consistent with a richer mix of systems and larger cells. It is also consistent with customers paying more per unit for compliance, not for scarcity.

<!-- k:#6B6558|PART 03 · THE SUPPLY PIVOT -->
## The factories are moving faster than the demand

The storage pivot is easiest to see in Korea, because the reallocation is physical.

Samsung SDI is converting the Synergy Cells plant in the United States into an ESS battery base and has started LFP output at StarPlus. LG Energy Solution is adding ESS capacity at its Honda joint venture and says its ESS order backlog reached about 160GWh at the end of September. SK On is moving Seosan and part of its Georgia car capacity to storage, and has signed LFP cathode supply deals with Korean materials firms L&F and POSCO Future M to support it.[^4][^7][^8]

In China, the product transition is running alongside the capacity build-out. 314Ah cells, a measure of cell capacity, remain the workhorse for utility projects, while 500Ah-plus products from CATL, EVE and others are being introduced faster than the older lines can be converted, producing shortages of the newest large cells even as older capacity sits idle.[^17]

The aggregate numbers are brutal. Industry association estimates put planned storage-cell capacity above 2,000GWh against roughly 850GWh of global demand expected in 2026. Sodium-ion is even more lopsided: BloombergNEF puts 2026 global sodium-ion capacity at 273GWh, about four times expected demand, with 98% of capacity located in China.[^17][^18]

This is the part of the story that the "storage boom" headlines miss. The pivot is a capital reallocation, and capital is arriving faster than demand can absorb it. That is why volume can grow by double digits while price and margin do not.

<!-- k:#8E97A8|PART 04 · WHY THE MARGIN DID NOT FOLLOW -->
## The squeeze is cost, tax and mix, not a classic price war

The intuitive explanation for weak battery profits is a price war. The data does not quite support it.

Chinese storage-cell prices bottomed near Rmb0.31/Wh at the end of 2025, rose to about Rmb0.365/Wh in August 2026 and stood at Rmb0.353/Wh on November 4, according to InfoLink, a Taiwan-based PV and battery price assessor. The August increase was driven by cost and tax, not demand: CATL raised its 314Ah list price by 2.2% on August 1, EVE added a 2% surcharge for the consumption tax from September 1, and more than ten smaller suppliers followed with increases of 5% to 30%. Inverter makers Huawei and Kehua also raised prices. By late October the cell price was slipping again.[^17][^19]

So the margin compression came from three directions at once. Costs rose, because lithium prices and tax both moved against manufacturers. Prices could not fully pass those costs through, because storage buyers are more price-sensitive than carmakers. And the mix shifted toward storage, which carries lower gross margins than premium EV cells. CATL's 20.7% quarterly gross margin is not a collapse. It is the arithmetic of a business growing fastest in its least profitable segment.[^1][^2]

Policy is now a structural cost, not a one-off. The consumption tax starts at 2% and rises to 4% on September 1, 2027. The export rebate falls to zero on January 1, 2027. Sodium-ion, solid-state and fuel-cell batteries are exempt from the consumption tax until the end of 2028, a deliberate tilt toward technologies the Chinese government wants to scale. For lithium-ion incumbents, the direction is clear: the state is withdrawing the support that made export margins look better than domestic ones.[^2][^25]

The cash-flow numbers matter as much as the income statement. CATL's receivables and inventories are growing faster than revenue, and its operating cash flow is shrinking. In a business where customers are utilities and project developers rather than carmakers, that is what growth looks like before it is collected. It also explains why the market punished the stock even as profit rose: the quality of the earnings, not the quantity, was the surprise.[^1]

The second-tier results show the same mechanism with less cushion. Companies with storage customers and overseas capacity, such as EVE and CALB, converted volume into profit. Companies without them, or in the middle of restructuring, did not. Gotion's loss was largely a fair-value and currency effect, but the market reads the headline. Sunwoda's near-zero profit was an accounting event, but it still lands in the same quarter as a 53% revenue increase.

The deeper change is in how storage projects get paid. In China, the shift from mandatory renewable co-location to capacity payments is the most important reform in years. Anhui province, one of the first to publish details, will pay a benchmark of Rmb165 per kW per year, pro-rated by duration against a six-hour reference: about Rmb55 for a two-hour system, Rmb110 for four hours, and the full amount at six hours or more. Payment starts in January 2027 and is recovered from industrial and commercial users. The province also imposes a monthly availability test: miss 98% of the planned discharge once and nothing happens; miss it twice and 10% of the monthly payment is cut; three misses cost half; four or more and the payment is gone.[^20]

That is a real improvement in bankability, and it is still not a guarantee. A non-official sensitivity analysis published by a Chinese storage trade publication found that a four-hour project with EPC costs of Rmb0.877/Wh and a 20-year life earns a 6.47% unlevered IRR if it meets every test. Change the denominator from six hours to eight, assume an 85% collection rate and a three-year policy horizon, and the IRR falls to 2.27%.[^20] The mechanism is better than what it replaced; the returns still depend on details the developer does not control.

The same shift is happening abroad, with different instruments. In the United States, the foreign entity of concern (FEOC) rules decide whether a project can claim the 30% investment tax credit at all, and the material assistance cost ratio (MACR) threshold rises from 55% in 2026 to 75% by 2030. US installations fell 7% year on year in the second quarter, with utility-scale down 8%, which the energy consultancy Wood Mackenzie attributes to supply-chain and policy uncertainty. In Europe, negative-price hours rose to 3.4% of the year in 2025, creating the arbitrage that storage needs, but local-content rules, the EU battery passport under the bloc's battery regulation, mandatory from February 2027, and customs scrutiny are raising the cost of Chinese equipment. Italy's MACSE storage-capacity auction cleared at about a third of its ceiling price, a reminder that capacity mechanisms can set the price as easily as they support it.[^21][^22][^23][^24]

The common thread is that storage revenue is becoming a financing problem rather than a manufacturing one. The margin accrues to whoever can underwrite availability, navigate eligibility and finance a 15-year asset, not to whoever has the cheapest cell. That is why Samsung SDI's US ESS order book and LGES's AMPC credit matter more than their cell prices, and why CATL's cost advantage does not automatically translate into a higher valuation.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The next war is over who gets paid

For five years the battery industry optimised for share. Capacity was the weapon: build faster than demand, lock in automakers, and let scale drive cost down. That bargain worked while cells were scarce and subsidies were rising. It stopped working in the third quarter of 2026, when revenue growth of 50% to 75% produced flat, falling or negative profit at many of the companies that reported.

The storage pivot is the right response, but it changes the basis of competition. In electric vehicles, the customer is a carmaker who buys on specification, volume and price. In storage, the customer is a developer or utility who buys on availability, warranty, grid compliance and financeability. The second sale is harder to win with cost alone, and it is much harder to keep when the project's revenue depends on a capacity mechanism or a tax credit.

Three tests will settle the next phase. First, can storage-cell prices hold above Rmb0.35/Wh once the tax-driven increase fades? Second, do the disclosed order books convert into deliveries, or do framework agreements quietly shrink? Third, do capacity-payment projects in China and subsidy-qualified projects in the United States clear their return thresholds, or does the financing cost eat the margin? None of these is a demand question. All three decide who earns a return.

CATL's problem is not that the world has stopped buying batteries. It is that the old trade, volume for margin, no longer clears at the valuation the market once paid for. The company is still the lowest-cost producer and still the largest. But the quarter showed that in a market where policy sets part of the price and storage sets the growth, scale alone is no longer a moat. It is now a working-capital requirement.
