CATL's third-quarter report reads like a misprint. Revenue rose 58.6% to Rmb165.2bn. Net profit rose 14.5% to Rmb21.3bn. The next morning the world's largest battery maker saw its Hong Kong shares fall 10.6%, and JPMorgan cut the stock to neutral.[^1][^2]

The market was not rejecting growth. It was repricing its quality. Gross margin fell to 20.7% in the quarter, the second consecutive decline, and to 22.7% for the first nine months from 25.3% a year earlier.[^1] China began levying a 2% consumption tax on lithium batteries on September 1; the export rebate had already been cut from 9% to 6% and disappears on January 1, 2027. At the same time the sales mix tilted toward grid storage, where cells compete on price and duration rather than brand.[^2]

That is the uncomfortable core of this earnings season. Demand is accelerating: BloombergNEF, the energy research arm of Bloomberg, expects 188GW/535GWh of global storage additions in 2026, up 45%.[^3] The pivot to storage is real. It is not repairing margins. In many cases it is compressing them.

## Everyone grew. Almost no one kept it

The quarter was a clean natural experiment: the same demand shock, applied to companies with different positions in the chain and different exposure to policy.

Eight of the nine grew revenue by more than 50%. Profit went in three directions.

CATL's problem is arithmetic. Revenue grew four times faster than net profit, and the gap appeared in the balance sheet too: operating cash flow fell 23.6% in the first nine months, receivables rose 47.8% and inventories rose 53.2%. Growth at this stage consumes working capital faster than it produces earnings.[^1]

Korea's three cellmakers show the same demand producing different outcomes. LG Energy Solution, the country's largest, lifted operating profit 25.7%, but KRW416.9bn of its KRW756bn came from the US advanced-manufacturing production credit, known as AMPC; without it the margin is 3.7%, and the company still posted a net loss of KRW296.9bn.[^4][^5][^6] Samsung SDI, the second, returned to profit with operating profit of KRW407.8bn, its best in 12 quarters, on storage orders above 60GWh.[^7] SK On stayed in operating loss at KRW80.5bn, though narrower than a year earlier.[^8] Panasonic Energy, the Japanese battery arm, grew first-half revenue 46.9% and nearly tripled operating profit.[^9]

China's second tier is where the divergence becomes violent. EVE Energy and CALB turned 50%-plus revenue growth into 60% to 97% profit growth; Gotion High-tech, the Volkswagen-backed cellmaker, grew revenue 74.6% but reported a Rmb537m net loss, or a Rmb145m adjusted profit once fair-value and currency swings are stripped out; Sunwoda's profit fell 98% after it deconsolidated its power-battery subsidiary.[^10][^11][^12][^13]

This is not a story of good and bad companies. It is three profit models hitting the same demand curve: a scale leader absorbing policy costs, Korean incumbents monetising US eligibility, and Chinese challengers whose reported earnings depend as much on accounting events as on cell margins.

<!-- k:#5A6B9E|PART 02 · THE DEMAND IS REAL -->
## The boom is real. The order book is softer

The demand side is not in doubt. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, a UK battery supply-chain researcher, counted 177GWh of grid-scale storage commissioned globally in the first eight months of 2026, up 35%, with China accounting for 61% of August's volume.[^14] CABIA, the China Automotive Battery Innovation Alliance, recorded 73.5GWh of storage-battery sales in September, up 104.4% year on year and equal to 33% of combined power- and storage-battery sales.[^1]

Orders are where the story gets slippery. CESA, the Energy Storage Application Branch of the China Industrial Association of Power Sources, an industry group whose database tracks company-disclosed overseas orders, counted 584.6GWh disclosed by 145 Chinese companies in the first nine months. The quarterly path looks explosive: 112.7GWh, 176GWh, then 295.9GWh.[^15]

One contract explains most of the acceleration. On September 17, EVE Energy signed a 206GWh multi-year cell-supply agreement with Fluence, the US storage integrator. That single deal is 35.2% of all disclosed capacity in the nine months. Remove it and third-quarter orders were 89.9GWh, down about 49% from the second quarter.[^15] Signed capacity is not delivered capacity, revenue or cash.

The export data adds a second caution. China exported US$78.8bn of lithium-ion batteries in the first nine months, up 42.2% by value and 26.4% by volume. But exports to the United States fell 17.4%, while exports to the Netherlands rose 89.3%; the association attributes the Dutch jump to storage cells transiting Rotterdam.[^16] Value is growing faster than volume, which is consistent with a richer mix of systems, and with customers paying more for compliance rather than scarcity.

<!-- k:#6B6558|PART 03 · THE SUPPLY PIVOT -->
## The factories are moving faster than the demand

The storage pivot is easiest to see in Korea, because the reallocation is physical. Samsung SDI is converting its US Synergy Cells plant into an ESS base and has started LFP output at StarPlus, its joint venture with Stellantis, the European carmaker. LG Energy Solution is adding ESS capacity at its Honda joint venture and says its ESS backlog reached about 160GWh. SK On is converting 3GWh at Seosan and part of its Georgia car capacity to storage, backed by a 9GWh LFP supply deal with NeoVolta, a US storage developer.[^4][^7][^8]

In China, the product transition runs alongside the capacity build-out. 314Ah cells remain the workhorse for utility projects, while 500Ah-plus products are being introduced faster than older lines can be converted, leaving the newest large cells short even as older capacity sits idle.[^17]

The aggregate numbers are brutal. Planned storage-cell capacity exceeds 2,000GWh against roughly 850GWh of global demand expected in 2026. Sodium-ion is more lopsided still: 273GWh of capacity, about four times demand, with 98% in China.[^17][^18] The pivot is a capital reallocation, and capital is arriving faster than demand can absorb it.

<!-- k:#F0A832|PART 04 · THE DURATION QUESTION -->
## Why storage is measured in hours

The number that now shapes storage economics is not cell price but duration: energy capacity divided by power. A 100MW/400MWh system runs four hours at full power; a 100MW/200MWh system runs two. Most utility projects are configured at two or four hours, four hours has become the default in the United States and increasingly in China, and six hours or more is the territory of long-duration technologies.

China's policy has moved deliberately toward longer duration. Until 2025, most provinces required new renewable projects to attach storage, usually two hours, whether or not the grid needed it. In February 2025 the 136 document ended mandatory co-location and pushed renewable output into the market. In January 2026 the 114 document created a national framework for grid-side independent storage capacity payments, explicitly linking them to discharge duration and peak contribution.[^25] By October, provincial rules had turned that into specifics: Anhui pro-rates against the longest net-load peak of the year, currently set at six hours, and Qinhuangdao, a city in Hebei province, gives four points to four-hour-plus systems, two to two-to-four hours and zero below two.[^26]

Anhui's design is the clearest. It pays Rmb165 per kW per year, pro-rated by duration against a six-hour reference: about Rmb55 for a two-hour system, Rmb110 for four hours, and the full amount at six hours or more. Payment starts in January 2027 and is recovered from industrial and commercial users. A monthly test cuts the payment if discharge availability falls below 98%.[^20]

The six-hour reference is the policy choice that matters. A capacity payment buys firm power, not energy; six hours approximates a period of peak scarcity, and the cap avoids overpaying very long assets. Because the payment is linear, 165 divided by six works out to about Rmb27.5 per kWh of installed energy per year up to six hours, and less beyond. Duration is now a revenue variable: a two-hour system can run four hours at half power, but it gets credit for two.

That changes what developers build. Four hours captures most of the capacity payment while keeping lithium's cost advantage; six hours maximises the payment but requires more capital; beyond six hours, the payment per kWh falls and long-duration technologies have to compete on other merits. The 2,000GWh capacity overhang is therefore not just too much supply. It is supply aimed at a duration the mechanism is still defining.

<!-- k:#8E97A8|PART 05 · WHY THE MARGIN DID NOT FOLLOW -->
## The squeeze is cost, tax and mix, not a classic price war

Chinese storage-cell prices bottomed near Rmb0.31/Wh at the end of 2025, rose to about Rmb0.365/Wh in August 2026 and stood at Rmb0.353/Wh on November 4, according to InfoLink, a Taiwan-based PV and storage price assessor. The August increase was cost- and tax-driven, not demand-driven: CATL raised its 314Ah list price 2.2%, EVE added a 2% surcharge for the consumption tax, and more than ten smaller suppliers followed with increases of 5% to 30%. By late October the price was slipping again.[^17][^19]

So margin compression came from three directions: costs rose, because lithium prices and tax moved against manufacturers; prices could not fully pass those costs through, because storage buyers are more price-sensitive than carmakers; and the mix shifted toward storage, which carries lower gross margins than premium EV cells. CATL's 20.7% quarterly gross margin is not a collapse. It is the arithmetic of growing fastest in the least profitable segment.[^1][^2]

Policy is now a structural cost. The consumption tax starts at 2% and rises to 4% in September 2027; the export rebate disappears in January 2027. Sodium-ion, solid-state and fuel-cell batteries are exempt from the consumption tax until the end of 2028, a deliberate tilt toward technologies the Chinese government wants to scale.[^2][^24]

The cash-flow numbers matter as much as the income statement. CATL's receivables and inventories are growing faster than revenue, and operating cash flow is shrinking. That is what growth looks like before it is collected. It also explains why the market punished the stock even as profit rose: the quality of earnings, not the quantity, was the surprise.[^1]

Abroad, the same shift is underway. In the United States, FEOC rules decide whether a project can claim the 30% investment tax credit, and installations fell 7% in the second quarter as developers waited on policy.[^21][^22] In Europe, negative-price hours rose to 3.4% of the year in 2025, but local-content rules and customs scrutiny are raising the cost of Chinese equipment.[^23]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The next war is over who gets paid

For five years the battery industry optimised for share. Capacity was the weapon: build faster than demand, lock in automakers, and let scale drive cost down. That bargain worked while cells were scarce and subsidies were rising. It stopped working in the third quarter of 2026, when revenue growth of 50% to 75% produced flat, falling or negative profit at many of the companies that reported.

The storage pivot is the right response, but it changes the basis of competition. In electric vehicles, the customer buys on specification, volume and price. In storage, the customer buys on availability, warranty, grid compliance, duration and financeability. The second sale is harder to win with cost alone, and much harder to keep when project revenue depends on a capacity mechanism or a tax credit.

Three tests will settle the next phase. Can storage-cell prices hold above Rmb0.35/Wh once the tax-driven increase fades? Do disclosed order books convert into deliveries? Do capacity-payment projects clear their return thresholds? None is a demand question. All three decide who earns a return.

CATL's problem is not that the world has stopped buying batteries. It is that the old trade, volume for margin, no longer clears at the valuation the market once paid for. Scale alone is no longer a moat. It is now a working-capital requirement.

## Three dates

On January 1, 2027, China's battery export rebate falls to zero. On February 18, 2027, the EU battery passport becomes mandatory.
