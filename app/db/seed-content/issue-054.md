On September 1, China's decade of tax-free batteries ended. Under Announcement No. 20 of 2026 — signed on July 16 by the Ministry of Finance, the General Administration of Customs and the State Taxation Administration — five categories of battery now carry a 2% consumption tax: mercury-free primary cells, nickel-metal hydride batteries, lithium primary cells, lithium-ion batteries, and all-vanadium flow batteries. On September 1, 2027, the rate rises to 4%. The same document begins taxing solar cells in 2027, at 2% then 4%. And in a third paragraph, it names who pays nothing at all.[^1][^3]

The invoice arrived faster than the tax did. CATL moved its 314Ah storage cell from RMB 0.414/Wh to RMB 0.423/Wh. EVE Energy told customers that from September 1 it would add a 2% consumption-tax surcharge to the base price of every battery product sold at home, and that export-bound goods would be eligible for a rebate, keeping domestic and export prices in line. Lishen Battery, a Tianjin-based cell maker, passed on more: the 2% plus a 7% urban maintenance and construction tax and a 5% education surcharge. Smaller producers Anhui Eagoal and Yili New Energy issued notices of their own; Desay Battery, a Shenzhen-listed battery manufacturer, told investors on September 7 that its Hunan storage-cell lines were running flat out.[^3]

In absolute terms the number is small. At an average cell price of RMB 0.36/Wh, 2% is about RMB 0.007/Wh — roughly RMB 7 million for every GWh — by the estimate of Li Yisha, a storage analyst at Shanghai Nonferrous Metals (SMM), the Chinese metals pricing agency. What makes it land is the market underneath: GGII, a Chinese battery-industry research house, has the average 314Ah cell climbing from about RMB 0.28/Wh in early 2025 to about RMB 0.38/Wh by mid-2026, average utilisation across the storage-cell sector is above 90%, and mainstream 314Ah cells are tight. As Envision AESC, Envision Group's battery unit, put it, the tax was the trigger, but the pricing power that let makers pass it on came from a supply-demand balance that has finally turned their way.[^3]

That is the bill. The more telling part of the document is the list of who is exempt.

<!-- k:#2F6E77|PART 01 · THE BILL -->
## A price tax, not a profit tax

Consumption tax does not work like the value-added tax the industry is used to. VAT runs down the chain and is credited back at each link; the consumption tax is embedded in the price and lands on the producer. Sell a cell for RMB 100 before VAT, and 2% is RMB 2 — but that RMB 2 does not travel down the supply chain as a credit in the ordinary way. If the selling price cannot move, it comes out of gross margin. In 2027, when the rate doubles to 4%, it comes out twice as hard.[^1]

The document is careful not to tax the same cell twice. A producer that buys, or takes back from toll processing, or imports an already-taxed battery and uses it to make another taxable battery may deduct the tax already paid, in proportion to the quantity consumed in the period. A producer that makes a taxable cell and feeds it straight into another taxable product does not pay at that internal transfer. Imports fall under the tax from September 1 too — and because import VAT is calculated on a base that includes consumption tax, a 2% tax nudges up the VAT base as well.[^1][^5]

For the companies that dominate Chinese storage, the point is less the 2% than the fact of it. For a decade the tax code treated batteries as a category to be encouraged. Now it treats lithium-ion as a business that has arrived. EVE said it was working through its supply chain to share and pass on the incremental cost; tier-one makers, with share and customer loyalty, have the leverage to do so, while smaller firms face the old choice of losing orders or eating the loss.[^3]

<!-- k:#B0432F|PART 02 · THE EXEMPT LIST -->
## Sodium and solid-state pay nothing until 2029

Article three of Announcement No. 20 exempts, from September 1, 2026 to December 31, 2028, sodium-ion batteries, solid-state batteries, fuel cells, and the perovskite, tandem and gallium-arsenide variants of solar cells. The lithium-ion battery — the technology China has industrialised at a scale no one else can match — pays. The next generation does not.[^1]

There is a condition attached, and it is the kind that decides who benefits. To claim the exemption, a product must meet its national standard; where no national standard exists, the exemption does not apply. Before the first exempt filing, the producer must obtain a test report from a lab holding a valid CMA (China Metrology Accreditation) certificate with the relevant battery tests in its scope. In other words, the tax holiday is not a technology label a company can claim for itself — it is a standard it has to prove. For sodium and solid-state makers still writing those standards, that is a real gate.[^1][^5]

Read the list and the policy has a shape. China has spent this year telling its battery industry that the era of racing to add capacity is over — the production-warning mechanism, the campaign against the price war, the cost-accounting notice from the pricing authority, all in the same season. The consumption tax is the same message in the language of revenue: the state will stop subsidising the mature technology, and keep subsidising the one it wants to win next. Regular readers have met this clock before: the 2027 solid-state consensus (No. 046) put sample-scale production two years out. A tax exemption that runs to the end of 2028 is that timeline, written into fiscal law.[^1][^5]

<!-- k:#5A6B9E|PART 03 · THE OTHER CLOCK -->
## The export rebate is being cut, not the consumption tax

The instinct is to read the new consumption tax as a brake on exports. The document points the other way. Announcement No. 2 of 2026, issued in January, cut the VAT export rebate on battery products from 9% to 6% for the period April 1 to December 31, 2026, and cancels it entirely from January 1, 2027. Solar products lost their VAT export rebate outright from April 1. But article three states that for the products in question that are subject to consumption tax, the export consumption-tax policy is unchanged: rebate and exemption continue to apply.[^2]

That is the two-clock problem in one industry. On the VAT side, the support is being withdrawn: the export rebate on battery products, already cut from 9% to 6% for 2026, ends altogether in 2027, leaving more of the input tax stranded in export cost. On the consumption-tax side, the destination principle holds — tax is charged where goods are consumed, not where they are made — so an exporter that paid the tax upstream can recover it, under the formula in Announcement No. 11 of 2026: the rebate equals the tax base multiplied by the rate. Which is exactly the mechanism EVE described to its customers: charge 2% at home, rebate it on the way out, keep the two prices level.[^2][^4][^6]

So the correct reading is narrower than "Beijing is taxing exports." It is taxing domestic sales of lithium batteries, keeping the consumption tax neutral at the border, and steadily withdrawing the VAT support that made exports cheaper. The two moves point in opposite directions on paper; on the factory floor they add up to the same thing — a battery sold at home now carries a tax it did not carry last month, and a battery sold abroad is losing the support it counted on last year.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: the tax code is the roadmap

A 2% tax is easy to dismiss as noise, and the dismissal misses what a tax code is for. A subsidy is a promise; a tax is a decision about which activities the state believes can stand on their own. By ending a decade of exemption for lithium-ion while writing a three-year exemption for sodium, solid-state and fuel cells, Beijing has said — in the one document that costs it money to publish — which technology it considers grown, and which it still considers a seedling.[^1]

There is a second reason to read it closely. The same season produced the production-capacity warning mechanism, the campaign against the price war, and the cost-accounting notice aimed at below-cost selling. Taxing the mature technology and sparing the next one is the fiscal half of that programme: it raises the cost of doing business the old way and lowers the cost of doing it the new way, without a single administrative order. For an industry that has spent three years hearing that growth at any price is finished, the tax return is where that sentence becomes arithmetic.

The export rebate will be the test. China is not taxing its way out of the export market — it is withdrawing the VAT support that made the last decade cheap, while keeping the consumption tax neutral at the border. The companies that win the next phase will be the ones whose margins depend on neither.
