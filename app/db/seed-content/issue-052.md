By late August, the largest battery-storage site in Australia's National Electricity Market (NEM) is being built by an American infrastructure fund, a GE spinoff and China's biggest cell maker — and the cells are Chinese. Quinbrook Infrastructure Partners, the US energy-transition investment firm, has tapped GE Vernova, the US power-equipment company spun out of General Electric, for power conversion, control and integration on Stage 3 of Supernode, its flagship battery project in Queensland, while CATL 宁德时代 has supplied the battery system across all three stages. When Stage 3 lands, the campus reaches 780 MW / 3,075 MWh — the largest operating battery in the NEM, financed to about AU$1.2 billion across the three stages.[^1]

That is the part that is easy to say. The harder part is the market around it. Japan capped China's cells at thirty percent by rule; Europe wrapped them in tariffs and subsidy walls. Australia did neither. It threw open the door, let the cells in, and then relied on something far more unforgiving than a policy line: a wholesale market where battery price spreads have fallen 85% in a single year, where the frequency-ancillary-services market that used to underwrite every battery is heading toward zero, and where investor return expectations have jumped from 8–10% to 13–14%. Australia is the purest test of whether Chinese cells can survive a market — not a rule.[^2][^3]

## A site with no wall, and no subsidy

Supernode is deliberate. It sits on constrained industrial land next to the South Pine substation north of Brisbane, which is why CATL's EnerC Plus container system — designed for back-to-back installation, footprint about 20% smaller than its predecessor — matters as much as its chemistry. CATL supplied EnerC Plus for Stages 1 and 2 and its newer TENER S system for Stage 3. Stages 1 and 2, at 520 MW / 1,858 MWh, are now fully in commercial operation; **Stage 3 will add 260 MW / 1,216 MWh (a four-plus-hour duration)**, and it has already accepted clearance under the Generator Performance Standards (GPS), the grid-connection gate for Australia's NEM.[^1][^4]

There is a name for why China's cells are here, and it is worth being precise about it. Quinbrook did not pick CATL because Australia has a tariff wall making foreign cells cheap by comparison, or because a subsidy favours them. It picked CATL because the site needs what CATL sells — a standardised platform across all three stages, a long-term service agreement covering condition monitoring, performance tracking and preventive maintenance, and the capacity to close AU$469 million in Stage 3 debt on the strength of that. GE Vernova, in turn, brings what the site and the grid now need: its first grid-forming battery project in Australia, which extends the plant past plain energy storage into frequency support and system strength, the services conventional power plants used to supply. That is the deal. The grid has stopped buying megawatts; it is buying the things megawatts used to do for free.[^1]

## The freest market is the one that pays the least

Here is the twist Australia hands a Chinese cell maker. There is no rule that caps you, which is the good news — and no subsidy that cushions you, which is the bad news. The market does the allocating, and right now the market is brutal. AEMO, the Australian Energy Market Operator, reported that NEM-wide battery price spreads fell 85% in a single year, to an average of AU$51/MWh (about US$35/MWh) in the second quarter of 2026, even as grid-scale battery output passed 9,000 MW for the first time.[^2]

The Australian Energy Regulator (AER) put the shape on it. At the start of 2021 the NEM had five utility-scale batteries totalling 261 MW, most earning their keep from frequency control ancillary services (FCAS). By the end of 2025 that had become 6.1 GW, and nearly two-thirds of installed capacity now runs at least two hours. In 2025, batteries set the wholesale price 16.3% of the time — up from around 1% four years earlier — and 25.5% of evening-peak intervals in Queensland. FCAS costs, once the bedrock of every battery's business case, fell to their lowest level since 2016. The market's own report card is that storage grew so fast it cannibalised the revenue that justified building it.[^3]

That is the counterintuitive core of the Australian story, and it is why the project — not the rule — is the ceiling. The operators know it. At the Battery Asset Management Summit in Sydney, Thomas Dargue of Eku Energy, the Australian battery-owner and operator, described storage contracts shifting from "physical tolls," where you build a battery and eat the market risk, toward commoditised, interchangeable products that trade more easily; on Ascera Energy's Riverina project, 70% of the parties in the market sounding were not even energy users — they were financial intermediaries. Neoen's Jeremy Lloyd, the France-headquartered renewables developer, put it bluntly: FCAS has died, and every new wave of batteries pushes the value of the existing ancillary streams toward zero. And the money has responded — Silvia Torgoman of Ascera Energy said a project that would have cleared at 8–10% returns is now uninvestable, because investors expect 13–14%.[^5][^6]

## What survives: system strength and a thinner, sharper revenue stack

So who survives a market with no friendly rules? Not the operator who built a business case on FCAS and fast coal retirements. Matt Grover of Fluence, the American energy-storage system integrator whose team bids batteries into the NEM, framed the squeeze as cyclical — "a short-term market cycle that will come back around" — but he tied the pain directly to coal closures delayed beyond the three-year-old expectations that underpinned those cases. Thomas Schmitz of Aquila Clean Energy, the Asia-Pacific renewables investment platform, argued the real problem sits in project-finance structure rather than battery economics, comparing a battery to a peaker that has historically run at capacity factors of 1–2%, and warning against leaning on FCAS forecasts that cannot even be reconciled for the past month.[^6][^7]

The survivors do two things. First, they stack revenue that does not cannibalise itself. Torgoman's point on system strength is the sharpest: unlike FCAS, which structurally works by fining one participant to pay another, a system-strength contract does not eat your energy revenue — you can sell the energy contract on top. That is exactly what a grid-forming battery with GE Vernova's control and long-duration hardware is built to sell. Second, they reach for the longer horizon. The first eight-hour battery entered the NEM in June 2026, under New South Wales' Energy Road Map, and AEMO's reliability outlook flags that the real gap is not energy adequacy but system security — inertia and voltage support as inverter-based generation replaces coal. That is the ticket CATL and GE Vernova hold: long-duration and grid-forming, the two things a market with collapsing spreads and retiring coal will pay for, because there is no substitute for them.[^1][^3][^7]

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## The take: the market is the ceiling

The last issue this page argued the storage clock runs on policy. Japan proved it — a 30% written cap, and Chinese capital crossed the line while Chinese cells did not. Australia is the opposite pole, and it is the cleaner experiment. There is no wall, no cap, no subsidy. There is only a market, and the market is asking a question China's cell makers have not been asked before: not can you make a cheap cell, but can you make a battery that earns in a market where arbitrage spreads fell 85%, FCAS is dying, and investors want 14%?

The answer, read from Supernode, is that Chinese cells can win the biggest project in the freest market — but only by selling what the market cannot get elsewhere: long-duration storage, grid-forming control, and the full-lifecycle service that unlocks billions in infrastructure debt. That is a different skill from shipping cheap megawatts. In Japan the ceiling was a number. In Australia the ceiling is a price, and the price is still falling.
