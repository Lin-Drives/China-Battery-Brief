In Q1 2026 the world deployed 244.6 GWh of EV batteries, up 9.1% year on year. CATL 宁德时代 shipped 99.5 GWh — 40.7% of everything — and the combined CATL-BYD share over January–May ran to 54.6%. Add seven Chinese names in the top ten and the picture stops being a race: it is a consolidated duopoly with a state-subsidized tail. This issue reads the scoreboard — the share shifts, the price moves, and the money flows that actually decide where the industry goes next.

## Seven of the top ten are Chinese, and what it means

The headline number is CATL 宁德时代 at 40.7% of global EV battery usage in Q1 2026, up from 37.9% for full-year 2024. On its own that is a slow grind — under three points over five quarters. But the second-order effects are where the story lives. BYD 比亚迪 volumes fell 8.0% year on year in Q1, to 33.5 GWh, as its Chinese-market pivot kept squeezing and its European entry stayed hostage to the tariff talks. The Korean trio — LG Energy Solution, SK On and Samsung SDI — slipped to a combined 15.6%, down 2.1 points.[^1]

The aggregate is more striking than any single line. Seven of the top ten makers are now Chinese, and over January–April the Chinese cohort held 72.2% of global usage. For a fund or an OEM strategy desk, the conclusion is not that "China wins" — it is that the relevant comparables have narrowed to two. When two firms hold a majority and the next five are also domestic, vendor risk and pricing power become the same conversation.

There is a counter-argument worth holding on to. The share data is volume, not value; and it is measured in the batteries that shipped, not the margin they earned. BYD's volume dip is partly a deliberate mix shift into higher-margin segments. The Korean trio's slide is concentrated in the segments — cylindrical, premium NMC — where Chinese cells have not yet fully penetrated. The oligopoly is real, but it is an oligopoly in the middle of the market.[^2]

## LFP at $52, and the price floor that keeps sliding

Cell prices tell a quieter version of the same consolidation. LFP cells have settled in the low $50s per kWh — down roughly 1.9% in a single week on our ticker — and the long-run cost curve keeps sliding as cathode capacity floods in. Two forces are now at play that a pure market model would miss.

First, the cost curve has become a policy instrument. The §45X advanced-manufacturing production credit — $35/kWh for cells, $10/kWh for modules — anchors a price floor for anything built in the United States, while the new PFE (prohibited foreign entity) licensing rules decide whether Chinese technology can collect that credit at all. A subsidy floor and a political ceiling are now the same regulatory document. Second, LFP's share of global EV deployment reached roughly 55% in 2025 — the chemistry is no longer the "budget option," it is the default, and the economics have pulled sodium-ion into the picture as the next price-floor candidate.[^3]

Sodium-ion deserves a second look precisely because it resets the price floor. CATL's Naxtra 钠新 cells deliver ~175 Wh/kg at scale, roughly 30% below LFP pricing, and they are the first sodium chemistry to pass China's GB 38031-2025 safety standard. A record 60 GWh three-year sodium-ion supply agreement with the energy-storage company HyperStrong, signed April 2026, shows the storage segment will pay for a cheaper price floor before the automotive one does. Watch sodium as the low-cost wedge into stationary storage, not the next EV chemistry.[^4]

## Capex is the tell

The scoreboard that matters for the next decade is not this quarter's share — it is where the capex landed this year. The pattern across the 2026 money moves is consistent: Chinese firms are no longer exporting cells, they are exporting capacity, and they are structuring the financing to survive Western rule changes.

Debrecen is the flagship: CATL's €7.34bn Hungarian complex has its 40 GWh phase one sold out before the first cell shipped — a 2026 production ramp fully underwritten by customer contracts, not by CATL's own optimism. In Indonesia, the $6bn "Dragon" project is a joint venture: CATL leads it through its subsidiary CBL (Ningbo Contemporary Brunp Lygend), investing alongside two Indonesian state-owned entities — Indonesia Battery Corporation (IBC), the state holding platform Jakarta set up to keep the country's battery chain in national hands, and Antam, Indonesia's national nickel miner. In that structure CATL brings the technology, the capital and the operating know-how; Indonesia puts in the state platform and the mine, and in exchange takes equity in — not ore orders from — the whole nickel-to-cell chain. The value is being booked in the chain, not the mine. In Portugal, CALB's Sines gigafactory took €350m of formalized state incentives and launched phase-one construction, the cheapest entry onto a Western EU shore. And EVE Energy, the Chinese battery maker, is committing up to RMB 8.65bn to storage capacity at its Kulim site in Malaysia — Southeast Asia as the export base for cells, not just cars.[^5]

Read together, the flows answer the question the share table cannot: the duopoly is not just winning the present volume; it is placing the capital that will define the next capacity cycle, in jurisdictions chosen for political durability rather than wage arbitrage. The funding terms — co-invested local partners, EU-authorized state aid, pre-sold capacity — are the real risk management. That is what a balance-sheet read of the industry looks like when you stop counting shipments and start following the money.[^6]

## The take: not a tip sheet, a map

This page is journalism, not investment advice — we track the flow, you own the trade. But three signals from the scoreboard are worth more than any price print, and they are durable.

Capacity booked ahead of production has become the industry's new currency: Debrecen's sold-out ramp is a contracts problem, not a technology problem, and it tells you where customer confidence actually sits. Equity in the supply chain has replaced ore sales as the Chinese standard — the Antam and IBC stakes in Indonesia are a statement about where value is being captured. And the Western rulebook, from §45X licensing clauses to the battery passport, is being rewritten around the structures that move Chinese money across borders — the Ford-CATL licensing test case will tell us whether the royalty era survives.[^7]

None of these is a prediction. They are the coordinates. The duopoly's real moat is not chemistry — it is that it has already spent the capex, signed the offtake, and placed the equity. Following the money is how you see the next map before it is drawn.
