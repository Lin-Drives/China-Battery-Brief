Democrats took both chambers in the November 3 midterms: at least 224 seats in the 435-member House, at least 53 in the 100-member Senate, seven net gains where four were needed.[^1][^2]

That is a majority. It is not a veto-proof one. Overriding a president takes two-thirds of each chamber: 290 votes in the House, 67 in the Senate. The Senate result leaves the party 14 seats short, with no new class of senators until 2028.

The market read it as a reprieve anyway. Twelve Chinese battery, storage and solar stocks rose 3.26% on average on November 4 against the previous close; LG Energy Solution rose 9.5% and Samsung SDI 11.3%, and Shenzhen's tech index outran Shanghai's.[^3] Those moves price a stretch without new restrictions. The five that already exist are still in force.

## A majority is not a veto-proof majority

The House count is not final: 78 districts remain undecided, and a clean sweep would take the Democratic caucus to 302, past the 290 needed to override a veto. A bare majority is 218 of the 435 seats; a veto override is 290.[^1] That arithmetic is hypothetical. The Senate's is not: only a third of the chamber was up, and the next tranche is not available until 2028.

So for the next two years, no bill that tightens or loosens the treatment of Chinese batteries can become law without the president's signature. The Congress that was elected on November 3 can hold hearings, issue subpoenas, and refuse money. It cannot write a rule and put it into force.

That distinction is the one most coverage of the result skips. The instruments that actually regulate Chinese batteries are not waiting on Congress.

| Instrument | Who moves it | Can this Congress change it alone? |
|---|---|---|
| Section 301 tariffs | Executive, under delegated statute | No |
| Export controls (BIS) | Executive | No |
| FEOC / 45X eligibility | Statute, implemented through Treasury and IRS guidance | No; needs a signature |
| Inverter and grid-equipment ban | FCC and an August 26 emergency order | No; oversight only |
| COINS technology list | Treasury rulemaking | No; consultation only |
| A new restriction by statute | Congress and president together | No |

![Two horizontal bars: 224 House seats won against the 290 needed to override a veto, and 53 Senate seats against 67](/figures/062-override-gap.svg "Figure 01 · A majority is not two-thirds")

All six rows end the same way. The four instruments that reach a battery were made by agencies and by a president, and they can be unmade the same way. The one instrument that could add batteries to a capital-screening list, a Treasury rulemaking, is also the one the election cannot stop.

<!-- k:#2F6E77|PART 02 · THE LEVERS -->
## The levers that are not votes

No. 060 described five instruments: tariffs, subsidy eligibility, grid equipment, capital screening and China's own export controls. The legal bases have not moved: Section 301 supplies the tariffs,[^4] FEOC rules the subsidy test,[^5] COINS the capital screen,[^6] and China's October 2025 controls the export side.[^7] All five are administered rather than voted on, and an election does not reset an agency's calendar.

The nearest decision is the reach of the August 26 emergency order. The Department of Energy has 120 days from that date to publish implementing rules, which puts them in late December, before the new Congress is seated. Those rules decide what counts as covered equipment at 69 kV and above, and therefore which projects can be built at all. The Federal Communications Commission stopped answering industry questions on August 20, so developers are planning against a definition they have not seen.[^8]

Federal money is still going into domestic battery materials. On November 5 the Department of War's Office of Strategic Capital signed a US$250m conditional loan commitment with 6K Inc, a US company, to scale domestic production of battery cathode materials.[^9] Congress funds that office, so a Democratic House can question how it lends: subpoena the officials who signed, slow the next round of commitments through appropriations, or write limits into law. None of that reaches a loan already signed, and any new law needs the president's signature.

The private sector has already repriced. US storage developers spent the autumn raising credit facilities and launching compliance services around FEOC and domestic-content rules, because the binding question for a project is no longer whether Chinese cells are cheap but whether they can be certified and financed.[^10]

The fifth lever sits in China. China's suspension of its own export controls on high-energy-density cells runs to January 10, 2027.[^11] If it lapses, the restriction that matters will have come from China's government, not America's. That is the one decision in this file the midterms cannot touch at all.

## Why the Koreans rose nearly three times as much as the Chinese

Korean cellmakers rose roughly three times as much as their Chinese peers on the same news, and the reason is exposure rather than sentiment. The United States is where LG Energy Solution and Samsung SDI have built their growth case: LG Energy Solution's third-quarter operating profit of KRW756bn included KRW416.9bn of advanced-manufacturing production credit. Its revenue for the quarter was KRW9.64tn, and Samsung SDI's operating profit was KRW407.8bn on storage orders above 60GWh.[^12][^13]

For the Chinese companies, the United States is a smaller and shrinking part of the business. China exported US$7.36bn of lithium-ion batteries to the United States in the first nine months of 2026, down 17.4% year on year, while exports to the Netherlands rose 89.3%, much of it cells transiting Rotterdam. By value, the United States is about 9% of China's lithium-ion battery exports.[^14] A policy change that matters enormously to the American market can therefore be worth a 3.26% mark on a Chinese portfolio and a 10% mark on a Korean one.

What the election cannot undo is the executive's own record. Since January 2025 the administration has cut or frozen roughly US$177bn in federal grants, and the Environmental Protection Agency has repealed its greenhouse-gas endangerment finding and stopped assigning monetary values to avoided deaths and illness in its regulatory analysis.[^15] A Democratic House can subpoena the agencies that did this. It cannot restore the money or the finding without the president who removed them.

The demand side carries a risk of its own. Data centres are now a growing part of US storage demand. Cleanview counts 159GW of planned data centres, 41% of the planned national pipeline, in states whose new governors support pausing projects. Another 69.2GW, 18% of the pipeline, sits in states whose winners want to change tax breaks or other rules.[^15] Every data-centre measure called so far in the 41 local ballots went against the projects. A slower build-out would lower future demand, not installed capacity. Korean cellmakers carry the risk more directly than Chinese exporters: the US exposure that lifted their shares is the exposure that suffers first if the pipeline slows.

<!-- k:#F0A832|PART 04 · THE TWO YEARS -->
## Hearings, appropriations, and a deadline China controls

The Democratic majority's leverage is real but negative, and it arrives in stages. From January, committee chairs can compel documents and testimony from the Treasury on COINS implementation, from the FCC on the inverter ban, and from the Department of Energy on the grid-equipment rules. From October 1, 2027, the House can refuse to fund programmes it dislikes. The first budget it can shape is fiscal 2028, which begins on October 1, 2027, after the next election campaign is already under way; the 12 annual spending bills are the lever.

None of that creates a policy. It slows one down, or raises its political price, or forces an agency to publish what it would rather not.

The decisions that bind the industry in the meantime are elsewhere. The Treasury's comment window on COINS closes on December 7.[^6] China's export-control suspension lapses on January 10, 2027, and whether it is extended is a decision for China's government rather than America's. The tariff schedule remains the president's to change, in either direction, at any time.

<!-- k:#6B6558|THE TAKE -->
## Time is the only thing that changed

For a Chinese battery exporter, the useful reading of the midterms is narrow and unglamorous. The probability of a new American restriction fell. The probability that an existing one is enforced differently did not. And the rules that decide whether a project in Texas or Arizona can use Chinese cells are still written by agencies, still applied by grid operators, and still audited by an administration that did not change.

That makes the next two years a period for implementation work rather than strategy resets: certification paths, ownership structures that clear FEOC, alternative routes into the American market through licensing rather than ownership. Companies that were waiting for a political signal received one, but it says the calendar is open, not that the door is.

The one thing the election did buy is time, and time is only worth what is done with it. Two years is roughly the permitting lead time of a single plant, or the certification cycle of a single inverter. It is not enough to build a supply chain. It is enough to decide whether to start one.

<!-- k:#F0A832|ON THE HORIZON -->
## Three dates

On December 7, 2026, the comment period on the Treasury's COINS rule closes; any later addition of batteries would need another rulemaking. On January 10, 2027, China's export-control suspension expires unless China extends it again. On October 1, 2027, the Democratic House gains its first real spending lever, at the start of fiscal 2028.
